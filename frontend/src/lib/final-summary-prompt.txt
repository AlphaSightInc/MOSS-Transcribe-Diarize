Create the sole final briefing for a thoughtful reader who will not read the transcript.

GOAL
Deliver unusually high signal: the central takeaway, the most useful insights and mental models,
the reasoning or mechanism behind them, their implications, and any important but
under-appreciated facts or metrics. Optimize for understanding and decision value, not coverage.

QUALITY PRIORITIES
1. Grounding: use only transcript-supported content. Do not infer unstated facts, identities,
   motives, causality, or consensus.
2. Epistemic accuracy: preserve whether something is an observation, a speaker's argument, a
   forecast, a hypothesis, an open question, or a preview. Attribute contested or evaluative
   claims. Never upgrade uncertainty or turn a question into an answer.
3. Insight: explain why a supported idea matters and how its reasoning works. Do not invent a
   deeper lesson because two facts are adjacent. If the transcript is setup-only, say that it
   frames an open question and does not answer it.
4. Selectivity: omit repetition, sponsor material, show logistics, and colorful anecdotes unless
   they materially explain the central takeaway.

SOURCE AND CENTRALITY TEST
- Keep narrator/interviewer setup, a guest's claims, and reported outcomes distinct. Do not fuse
  separate transcript sections into a causal story unless the transcript explicitly connects
  them. Sequence alone is not causation.
- Include a fact only if removing it would materially weaken the reader's understanding of the
  central question, mechanism, disagreement, or implication. Novelty and color are not enough.
- Judge importance by explanatory leverage, not by how many lines a fact occupies or how
  surprising it sounds. Opening banter, announcements, historical curiosities, and event details
  are not key takeaways unless later reasoning depends on them.
- In a comparison, do not infer shared premises or goals from overlapping vocabulary; report
  only explicit agreement and otherwise present the contrast.

SILENT WORKFLOW BEFORE WRITING
1. Build an evidence map: for each candidate takeaway, identify its speaker and classify it as
   stated fact, speaker argument, forecast, hypothesis, open question, or preview.
2. Rank candidates by explanatory value. Discard anything incidental or unsupported by a clear
   transcript passage.
3. Write from the surviving evidence using these patterns when applicable:
   - speaker argument: "X argues [claim] because [stated reasoning]."
   - comparison: "X argues A, whereas Y argues B." Do not add "both agree" unless they say so.
   - forecast: "X expects/may [outcome]" with the original uncertainty; never "will answer/prove."
   - setup-only: "The speakers frame [question]; this transcript does not provide the answer."
4. Check every causal verb (drives, causes, proves, demonstrates, leads to) against an explicit
   transcript connection. If none exists, remove or attribute it as a speaker's interpretation.
5. For a setup-only transcript, center the unanswered substantive question. Use [] for details
   and data references unless a quantitative anchor materially defines that question or its
   context; never fill them with banter, production/release logistics, or trivia.
6. Enforce cross-field relevance: every detail and metric must directly support a specific
   takeaway in summary or topics. Delete orphan facts that are merely mentioned, even if vivid or
   quantitative. After drafting, remove any item whose absence would not reduce explanatory value.

DATA-REFERENCE SELECTION PASS
- A data reference is any exact quantitative anchor that provides evidence: a count, percentage,
  ratio, multiple, money amount, rate, threshold, calendar year, duration, forecast horizon, or
  before/after change. Do not reserve this field for percentages or financial figures.
- Start from the completed summary and topics. For each selected claim, inspect only its supporting
  transcript passage and identify its exact quantitative anchors. If an anchor materially makes
  that claim more concrete, add it to data_references. In particular, preserve durations and
  forecast horizons that define a scientific transition, ratios that establish relative scale,
  and milestone years that locate a consequential business transition.
- Time is evidence when it changes the mental model. If a chosen claim contrasts a long-standing
  condition with a near-term transition, recover the transcript's exact historical duration and
  expected horizon even when the prose paraphrases them as "thousands of years" or "soon". Related
  values may share one entry when together they express a single trajectory or comparison.
- Each context must use the form "Supports '<topic title>': <explanation>" or
  "Supports the summary: <explanation>" and must copy a final topic title exactly when naming one.
  Exclude a quantity if it cannot be mapped to an actual final topic or summary claim; general
  relevance to the recording is insufficient.
- One to three entries is a useful default when qualifying anchors exist, not a quota. Do not add
  numbers from discarded banter, recording/release logistics, or anecdotes. A calendar year
  qualifies only when it locates a substantive historical or business transition, not the
  production history of this recording. Use [] when no selected takeaway benefits from a
  quantitative anchor. Increase coverage by finding useful evidence, never by lowering this bar.

OUTPUT
Return one raw, valid JSON object only. The first character must be { and the last must be }.
Use double quotes around every property name and string. Do not use markdown fences, comments,
trailing commas, or introductory text. Use exactly these five keys. This valid empty skeleton
shows the required top-level types; populate it with supported content:

{
  "summary": "",
  "topics": [],
  "details": [],
  "speaker_background": [],
  "data_references": []
}

Every topics element must be {"title": "...", "description": "..."}.
Every details element must be {"title": "...", "description": "...", "timestamp": "HH:MM:SS"}.
Every speaker_background element must be one string: "Name: explicitly stated role or background".
Every data_references element must be {"item": "...", "value": "...", "context": "..."}.
Never emit placeholder text, a missing value, or a colon without a complete JSON string before it.

FIELD INTENT
- summary: 1 to 2 concise sentences answering "What should the reader remember, and why does it
  change their understanding?" State conclusions directly; do not say the episode or briefing
  "explores" something.
- topics: a few mutually distinct mental models or explanatory chains. Each description connects
  claim -> mechanism or evidence -> implication. Do not repeat the summary.
- details: selected chronological support, not a transcript recap. Every item has a valid
  transcript timestamp and does not duplicate a complete topic. Use [] when the transcript has
  no substantive evidence beyond framing an open question.
- speaker_background: only explicitly stated roles/background; otherwise [].
- data_references: selective quantitative-evidence appendix at the end. Include only metrics that substantiate a
  major claim, reveal scale/change/trade-off, or materially improve understanding. "Important
  but under-appreciated" means high explanatory leverage, not merely unusual or memorable. Copy the
  complete value and unit when practical and explain its significance. Omit incidental metrics;
  use [] only when the selected takeaways have no qualifying quantitative anchor. Metric inclusion
  or coverage is never a pass/fail requirement.

Before returning, silently verify that JSON.parse would succeed, all five keys exist with the
shown types, every important claim is traceable to the transcript with its original certainty,
topics are distinct, every detail/metric supports a selected takeaway, metrics are selective
rather than exhaustive, and the prose is grammatically complete.
